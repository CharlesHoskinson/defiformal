                                        Abstract
                                        Decentralized Finance (DeFi) services are usually constructed by composing a variety of smart contracts.




arXiv:2606.05418v1 [cs.CR] 3 Jun 2026
                                        While composability is a key driver of the success of DeFi, it also creates security risks: adversaries may
                                        exploit interactions between newly deployed contracts and the pre-existing ones to inflict economic losses.
                                        We introduce MEV non-interference, a formal security notion for DeFi composability requiring that the
                                        maximal extractable value from a set of newly deployed contracts is not increased by interactions with the
                                        existing blockchain state. To support this notion, we define local MEV, a novel measure of economic attacks
                                        that focusses on the loss of a given set of victim contracts. We study two adversarial models, with bounded
                                        and unbounded wealth, and establish sufficient conditions and locality principles that enable modular rea-
                                        soning about secure composability. We apply the framework to representative DeFi compositions, including
                                        exchanges, AMMs, options, lending pools, routers, and arbitrage contracts, showing how it distinguishes
                                        secure compositions from vulnerable ones. Our results provide a formal foundation for reasoning about the
                                        economic security of DeFi compositions.
                                        Keywords: smart contracts, blockchain, decentralized applications, formal methods, verification



                                        1. Introduction

                                            Decentralized Finance (DeFi) is often described as “money Lego” because it enables the construction
                                        of complex financial services through the composition of simpler building blocks [1, 2]. Recent empirical
                                        analyses of the DeFi ecosystem confirm that DeFi protocols are deeply interconnected in practice, giving rise
                                        to highly intricate dependency structures and interactions [3, 4]. While such composability is a key driver
                                        of the success of DeFi, it also introduces significant security challenges: adversaries can exploit unintended
                                        interaction among protocols to obtain an economic profit to the detriment of honest users [5, 6]. These risks
                                        are further exacerbated by emerging platforms that allow users to seamlessly create arbitrary compositions of
                                        DeFi protocols [7]. This raises a fundamental and still largely unanswered question: under which conditions
                                        is a DeFi composition secure?
                                            Despite the clear practical relevance of this question, research on DeFi composability remains surprisingly
                                        limited. The first notion of secure DeFi composition in the scientific literature is the one introduced by Babel,
                                        Daian, Kelkar and Juels in their seminal “Clockwork finance” paper [8]. The focus of this work is on attacks
                                        in which adversaries exploit newly deployed contracts to increase their profit opportunities. Accordingly,
                                        their notion of composability requires that augmenting a blockchain state S with a set of contracts ∆ should
                                        not allow adversaries a to extract a substantially larger Maximal Extractable Value (MEV). In formulae,
                                        letting MEV(S ) denote the maximal value that adversaries can extract from S , and writing S | ∆ for the
                                        blockchain state S extended with the contracts ∆, their criterion is expressed as:

                                                                 ∆ is ε-composable in S        iff   MEV(S | ∆) ≤ (1 + ε) MEV(S )                            (1)

where ε parameterises the “not substantially larger” condition above. For example, let ∆ be a contract
that allows users to bet on the future price of a token, using an Automated Market Maker (AMM) in S
as a price oracle. The key issue is that the AMM prices are not fixed: they are determined by the current
token reserves held by the AMM and therefore fluctuate in response to trades. In particular, large trades can
induce substantial price fluctuations. If the adversary has sufficient capital in S , criterion (1) would correctly
classify ∆ as not 0-composable in S . Indeed, the adversary can strategically manipulate the AMM price by
executing large trades, thereby creating artificial price fluctuations that allow them to systematically win
the bet. As a result, the adversary can extract strictly more MEV from S | ∆ than from S alone.

Limitations of ε-composability. We argue that the ε-composability has some limitations.
    First, using the MEV of the entire blockchain state S as a baseline for comparison makes the security
guarantee provided by ε-composability difficult to interpret. One might naturally conclude that deploying
a contract ∆ in S is safe after establishing that ∆ is 0-composable with S . However, 0-composability only
guarantees that the total MEV of the extended state S | ∆ does not exceed that of S ; it does not constrain
where this MEV is extracted from. To illustrate, suppose that the total value of tokens in ∆ is equal to
the MEV of S , and that extracting value from ∆ eliminates the MEV opportunities previously available
in S . Then ∆ is still 0-composable with S , even though an attack on S | ∆ may drain the entire value
of ∆ (see Example 18 for a concrete scenario). From the perspective of the designer or users of ∆, such a
composition would hardly be considered secure.
    A second drawback of using the global MEV as a baseline is that (1) classifies as not composable contracts
whose MEV is intentional and does not arise from any interaction with the rest of the system. Consider, for
instance, an airdrop contract ∆ that allows anyone to withdraw its entire balance. Despite being completely
independent from the rest of the system, ∆ is not 0-composable with any S , since deploying it necessar-
ily increases the total MEV of the system, i.e., MEV(S | ∆) > MEV(S ). One could attempt to recover
composability by choosing a sufficiently large value of ε, but this is both cumbersome and conceptually
unsatisfactory. In the extreme case where MEV(S ) = 0, the contract is not ε-composable for any ε ≥ 0,
regardless of its complete independence from the rest of the system.
    Finally, the reliance on global MEV poses both algorithmic and usability concerns. From a computational
standpoint, evaluating (1) requires reasoning about a property of the entire blockchain state, which may be
prohibitively large in practice. From a mitigation standpoint, when ε-composability fails because additional
MEV can be extracted from contracts already present in S , it is unclear how the designer of ∆ should
react. The vulnerable contracts reside in the pre-existing state and are therefore outside the control of the
deployer. Consequently, the definition may identify a composability problem without providing actionable
guidance on how to address it.

Secure composability as non-interference. The limitations of ε-composability suggest the need for compos-
ability notions that do not rely on the global MEV of the blockchain state.
    Our starting point is the notion of non-interference, which has been extensively studied in information-
flow security since the 1980s [9, 10, 11, 12, 13]. In its classical form, non-interference requires that adversaries
interacting with a software system cannot observe private data. More precisely, the property holds when
public outputs (that can be observed by adversaries) are not affected by private inputs.
    We adapt this principle to the setting of smart contracts composition, ending up with a notion that we
call MEV non-interference. Intuitively, the contracts ∆ being deployed should not become more vulnerable
merely because they are composed with a larger blockchain state. In other words, the maximal economic
loss that adversaries can inflict on ∆ should only depend on the interactions of the adversary with ∆, and
not with the rest of the system. Under this interpretation, the interactions between the adversary and the
surrounding system S play the role of the private inputs in classic non-interference, while the economic loss
suffered by ∆ plays the role of the public outputs. MEV non-interference therefore requires that interacting
with contracts in S does not enable adversaries to cause more loss on ∆ than they could achieve by interacting
with ∆ alone.
    We argue that MEV non-interference captures the security property that protocol designers are naturally
interested in when deploying new contracts. By focusing on the local MEV extractable from ∆, our notion

overcomes the above-mentioned limitations of ε-composability. First, contracts whose MEV is intentional

under suitable conditions, one can establish the composability of ∆ by reasoning only about ∆ and its
dependencies, without analysing the entire blockchain state.
Contributions. This paper introduces a formal framework for reasoning about the secure composability of
smart contracts. We summarise our main contributions as follows:
   • MEV non-interference. We adapt the classical notion of non-interference from information-flow
     security to the smart contracts setting, obtaining a novel security criterion for protocol composition.
     To the best of our knowledge, this is the first application of non-interference principles to the study of
     smart contracts composability.
   • Local MEV. We introduce local MEV, a novel measure of economic attacks that quantifies the
     maximal loss that adversaries can inflict on a given set of victim contracts. In contrast to classical
     MEV [8, 14], which is defined for an entire blockchain state, local MEV focuses on the contracts whose
     security is under analysis. This shift from a global to a local perspective is the key ingredient enabling
     our notion of MEV non-interference.
   • Two adversarial models. We define two variants of local MEV. The first, MEV(S , C), models
     adversaries whose wealth is bounded by their capital available in the blockchain state. The second,
     MEV∞ (S , C), models adversaries with unbounded capital, capturing attacks enabled by mechanisms
     such as flash loans [15].
   • MEV non-interference. Building on local MEV, we introduce two notions of secure composability:
     S ̸⇝ ∆ for bounded-wealth adversaries and Γ ̸⇝∞ ∆ for unbounded-wealth adversaries (where Γ de-
     notes a system of contracts obtained by removing users’ wallets from the blockchain state). Intuitively,
     these properties require that composing ∆ with an existing blockchain state does not create additional
     opportunities to extract value from ∆.
   • Local reasoning principles. We establish locality results showing that both local MEV and MEV
     non-interference can be analysed by considering only a suitable fragment of the blockchain state. For lo-
     cal MEV, we identify conditions under which the maximal loss suffered by the victim contracts depends
     only on those contracts and their dependencies (Theorems 4 and 12 for bounded- and unbounded-
     wealth adversaries, respectively). Building on these results, we prove that MEV non-interference is
     preserved for suitable state narrowings (Theorems 15 and 18). Intuitively, we can remove from the state
     the non-dependencies of ∆, provided that the contracts in ∆ are sender-agnostic, i.e. their behaviour
     is uniform with respect to their callers. For bounded-wealth adversaries, we additionally require that
     the removed contracts do not induce token flows to ∆ or its dependencies. These locality principles
     provide both conceptual and algorithmic advantages over approaches based on global MEV.
   • Sufficient conditions for secure composability. We establish practical sufficient conditions for
     MEV non-interference (Theorems 14 and 17). A key ingredient is observational invariance, a property
     ensuring that adversarial interactions with the existing blockchain state cannot influence what the
     newly deployed contracts observe. Combined with suitable conditions on token flows, observational
     invariance guarantees that composing a contract with an existing system does not create additional
     opportunities for adversarial value extraction.
   • Analysis of representative DeFi compositions. We validate our framework on a collection of
     representative DeFi compositions, including exchanges, AMMs, binary options, lending pools, swap
     routers, and arbitrage protocols (Table 2). Using the sufficient conditions and locality principles devel-
     oped in the paper, we establish secure composability results for several classes of protocol compositions
     and identify concrete scenarios in which composability fails because of exploitable dependencies, token
     flows, or manipulable price oracles. The resulting classification highlights the practical usefulness of
     MEV non-interference as a tool for analysing real-world DeFi ecosystems.

2. Blockchain model
   We fix a model of account-based blockchains and smart contracts inspired by Ethereum. To keep our
theory general, we abstract from some of Ethereum’s peculiar features, such as its underlying consensus
protocol, gas mechanism, and EVM bytecode, just focussing on the high-level behaviour of smart contracts.
We discuss the abstractions of our model in Section 9.

Tokens. We assume a set T of token types (T, T′ , . . .), which represent the types of exchanged (fungible)
assets. While Ethereum handles its native crypto-currency (the ETH) differently from the custom assets
(smart contracts implementing the ERC-20 interface), we provide a uniform treatment of assets, handling
both ETH and other token types with the same set of operators.

Accounts. We assume a countably infinite set A of accounts, partitioned into:
   • user accounts A, B, . . . ∈ Au , which represent the active entities who can initiate transactions (the
     so-called “externally owned” accounts in Ethereum);
   • contract accounts C, D, . . . ∈ Ac , which represent the passive entities that can react to transactions
     initiated by user accounts.
    We use a, b, . . . as meta-variables ranging over both user accounts and contract accounts. We designate
a subset M of user accounts as adversaries, i.e., users with the ability to influence the selection and ordering
of transactions in the blockchain. In the Ethereum ecosystem, this subset includes, e.g., block proposers and
MEV searchers [16]. Although the analysis of a specific contract may depend on the particular choice of M,
there exist natural and robust choices — such as a finite set of fresh user accounts not already appearing in
the contract code or state — for which the resulting analysis is insensitive to this choice.

Wallets. A wallet w ∈ T → N is a finite map from token types to non-negative integers, quantifying the
amount of tokens held by an account. We represent a wallet as a comma-separated list of associations
between a token type T and its amount n, written as n: T. We denote by + the pointwise summation of
wallets.

Blockchain states. A blockchain state S is a finite map from accounts to their respective states. The state
of a user account is a wallet, while that of a contract account is a pair (w, σ), where w is the contract’s
wallet and σ is its persistent storage, represented as a key–value store. We denote by:
   • †S the set of contract accounts in S , i.e. †S = (dom S ) ∩ Ac ;
   • ωA (S ) the summation of the wallets of accounts A in S .
   Since the primary focus of this paper is the composability of contracts, it is often necessary to manipulate
blockchain states so as to add or remove accounts. This, in turn, requires a suitable notation for representing
the individual components of a state, as well as for constructing and deconstructing states in a systematic
manner. For this purpose, we adopt the following notation:
                                                       4
   • We denote by A[w] the state of a user account A whose wallet is w.
   • We denote by C[w, σ] the state of a contract account C whose wallet is w and storage is σ. A storage is
     represented as a comma-separated list of associations between keys and values, each written as k = v.
   • We denote by S | S ′ the composition of two blockchain states S and S ′ . Such composition is defined
     whenever dom S and dom S ′ are disjoint — namely, a blockchain state cannot contain multiple states
     for the same account.
   • We use W, W ′ , . . . to denote states comprising only user accounts.
   • We use Σ, Γ, ∆, . . . to denote states comprising only contract accounts.

Contracts. For generality, rather than assuming a specific syntax for the contract language, we model
contracts abstractly as public APIs whose methods have the following behaviour:

  1. receive parameters from the caller. In particular, the parameters include the immediate caller and the
     transaction initiator (these accounts may coincide);
  2. perform a finite sequence of effects drawn from the following kinds:
        • abort execution when specified conditions are not met;
        • read and update the contract storage;
        • send tokens from the contract to another account. Its behaviour depends on the whether the
          recipient is a user or a contract account:
             – If the recipient is a user account and the sender’s wallet contains a sufficient amount of tokens,
               then the tokens are immediately transferred to the recipient, possibly creating the account if
               it does not yet exist in the blockchain state.
             – If the recipient is a contract account, then the operation is interpreted as a mere approval of
               the transfer, while the actual transfer is triggered by an explicit receive action of the recipient.
        • receive tokens from the caller, provided that it has previously approved the transfer;
        • invoke methods of the same or of other contracts;
  3. return a value to the caller.
    Note that the aforementioned contract capabilities do not include the ability to directly inspect the state
of external accounts, including their wallets. This, however, is not a practical limitation. Dependence on a
user’s wallet can be enforced by requiring the caller of a method to provide tokens to the contract, while
dependence on the state of another contract can be realised by querying that state through its public getter
methods. For simplicity, we also forbid direct token transfers between user accounts. This restriction entails
no loss of generality, since such transfers can always be mediated through suitable contracts.

Contract dependencies. We say that a contract C is called by D if some method of D invokes a method of C.
We denote by ⊑ the reflexive and transitive closure of this relation. Given a set of contracts C, we denote by
deps(C) the set of dependencies of C, namely, the set of contracts called by some contract in C. Formally:

                                       deps(C) = {C′ | ∃C ∈ C. C′ ⊑ C}

We write deps(S ) as a shorthand for deps(†S ), i.e. the dependencies of the contracts occurring in S .

Well-formedness. We say that a blockchain state S = a1 [· · · ] | · · · | an [· · · ] is well-formed if (i) ⊑ is a
partial order and (ii) ∀i, D. D ⊑ ai =⇒ ∃j ≤ i. D = aj . Condition (i) requires that contracts can only call
previously deployed contracts or themselves. Condition (ii) means that all the dependencies of the contracts
in S are included in S , and if a contract C = ai calls D, then D appears on the left of C in the enumeration,
i.e. j ≤ i. Note that if S is well-formed, then:

   • S is closed under dependencies, i.e. deps(S ) ⊆ †S ;
   • mutual calls between contracts are forbidden;
   • when deconstructing S into S 0 | S 1 , the leftmost component S 0 is guaranteed to be well-formed, but
     the rightmost component S 1 is not, since some of the dependencies of S 1 may be in S 0 .

   Hereafter, we consider well-formed states up-to permutations of their individual components, provided
that the well-formedness condition is respected. Unless otherwise stated, in our statements we implicitly
assume that the blockchain states mentioned (in hypothesis or thesis) are well-formed.

Contract language. In our examples, we specify contracts in a Solidity-like language with primitive token
types. Namely, rather than implementing tokens as ERC-20 contracts, our language extends Solidity with
a base type token to represent token types. Each token type T supports the following operations:
   • T.send(rcv,n) has different behaviours depending on whether the recipient rcv is a user or a contract
     account. If rcv is a user account, then the operation transfers n:T from the contract to rcv. Instead,
     if rcv is a contract account, the operation authorizes the contract rcv to receive up to n:T from the
     caller’s wallet. We assume that a subsequent send with the same token and receiver overwrites the
     previous one, effectively cancelling it;
   • T.receive(n) transfers n:T from the immediate caller to the contract. The operation reverts if the
     caller has not previously approved the transfer, or if its wallet does not contain a sufficient balance of
     tokens T.

Sender-agnostic methods. We say that a contract method is sender-agnostic when the effect of an internal
call to the method does not depend on the identity of the caller. More precisely, consider two transactions
X0 and X1 with the same initiator, whose executions trigger internal calls to the method f of contract C:

                                           D0 : C.f(args)       D1 : C.f(args)

in the same state, with the same arguments, but with different senders D0 and D1 , both of which have
approved the token transfers needed to fund the respective calls. We then say that f is sender-agnostic
whenever the two internal calls either both revert, or both succeed producing the same effects, up-to the
transfer of tokens to the sender. More specifically, the two calls update the contract state in the same way,
transfer the same tokens to the same user accounts, perform the same internal calls to other methods, and
transfer the same tokens to their respective senders D0 and D1 .

call to setPrice depends on the sender: if it is the owner, the call succeeds, otherwise it reverts. Instead,
the method getRate is sender-agnostic, as its effect does not depend on the identity of the caller. For the
same reason, the method swap of the Exchange contract in Listing 2 is sender-agnostic as well.               ⋄

    In this section we introduce a notion of MEV tailored to the study of smart contract compositions.

adversary’s capabilities can be restricted while preserving MEV.

3.1. Definition
    We measure the effect of an attack as the variation in the wealth of a set of victim accounts before and
after the attack. Given a blockchain state S and set of accounts A (possibly comprising both user and
contract accounts), we define their wealth $A (S ) as the weighted sum of the tokens held in A’s wallets,
where the weights are the token prices. We denote by $1T the price of a token type T and assume that
$1T > 0 for all T. We assume these prices to be fixed throughout an attack (note instead that the prices
given by a PriceOracle contracts can vary, since they depend on the blockchain state).

  1. We measure the value that adversaries can drain from a given set of victim contracts C. This means
     that only the tokens extracted from the contracts in C contribute to the MEV. Unlike in (2), the tokens
     grabbed from other contracts do not contribute to MEV.

  2. We count as MEV all the tokens that M can remove from C, regardless of whether M can transfer them
     to their wallets. Namely, while (2) maximises M’s gain γM , our notion maximises C’s loss −γC . Doing
     so, our notion of secure composability also encompasses irrational adversaries who try to damage the
     victim contracts without necessarily making a profit.
  3. We parameterise our MEV w.r.t. the set D of contracts callable by M. This enables us to reinterpret the
     separation between private and public information in language-based non-interference. In that setting,
     the main insight is that observable (public) outputs remain unaffected by variations in confidential
     (private) inputs — equivalently, restricting inputs to the private ones must preserve the public outputs.
     In our context, we rephrase this by requiring that the loss inflictable to C (the public output) is not
     affected when restricting the set of callable contracts to a subset D (the private inputs).

   Theorem 2 establishes some basic properties of MEV:
   • Item 1 studies some border cases when the MEV is zero.

     the MEV: this is because maximising the loss of a contract may increase the gain of another one.

3.3. Front-running resistance
    Consider a state S where a user A is deploying new contracts ∆, with the expectation that the loss
inflictable to ∆ is bounded by MEV(S | ∆, †∆). This expectation is generally false, since an adversary could
front-run A’s deployment transaction with the deployment of new contracts Γ, and in this way increase the
loss inflictable to ∆. Theorem 5 gives sufficient conditions under which MEV is preserved by such front-
running attacks. This result will be the basis to establish front-running resistance for MEV non-interference.

   The proof of Theorem 5 leverages a fundamental MEV preservation result, established by Theorem 4:

   • the contracts in the boundary are sender-agnostic, i.e. they are not aware of the identity of the caller.

3.4. Observational invariance
    We now provide another MEV preservation result, which will be the basis to establish sufficient conditions
for MEV non-interference in Section 5.
    Theorem 6 states that the MEV inflictable to ∆ in S | ∆ is preserved by narrowing the callable contracts
to ∆ when ∆ is observationally invariant w.r.t. adversarial moves in S . Roughly, this means that any
adversarial transaction calling a contract in S cannot affect what ∆ observes by invoking the functions in S .
The observables include the returned values, the transferred tokens and the aborts of functions that can be
called from ∆. In other words, even if the adversary interacts with S , possibly changing its internal state,
the contracts in ∆ will have the same behaviour as if the adversary never interacted with S .
Definition 5 (Observational invariance). Let S | ∆ be a blockchain state (not necessarily well-formed). We
say that ∆ is observationally invariant in S whenever the following condition holds. Let S ′ be any state
reachable through moves of M from S | ∆. Then, we require that:

  1. if X ∈ κ(M) is such that callee(X) ∈ †S , then executing X in S ′ does not change the state of the
     contracts in ∆ (neither their wallet nor storage);
  2. if X, Y ∈ κ(M) are such that callee(X) ∈ †S , callee(Y) ∈ †∆, and the sequence XY is valid in S ′ , then
     Y satisfies the following conditions:

        • Y is valid in S ′ provided that the sender has enough tokens;
        • executing Y in S ′ triggers the same sequence of internal calls from the contracts in †∆ to those
          in †S , with the same arguments and sent tokens, as the sequence of calls that XY triggers in S ′ ;
        • each call triggered by Y has the same effects (of the kinds: aborts, token transfers, method calls
          to †∆, returns) of the corresponding call of XY.

5. MEV non-interference

   We formalise secure contract composability as a relation between blockchain states S and contract states
∆: intuitively, S ̸⇝ ∆ means that the adversary cannot leverage S to inflict more MEV to ∆ than it would
be possible by interacting with ∆ alone. We will then say that S is MEV non-interfering with ∆.

5.1. Definition
    Formally, MEV non-interference S ̸⇝ ∆ holds when the MEV extractable from the contract accounts
in ∆ (i.e., †∆) using any contract in S | ∆ is exactly the same MEV that can be extracted using only the
contracts in ∆. We write S ⇝ ∆ when S ̸⇝ ∆ does not hold.
Definition 8 (MEV non-interference). A state S is MEV non-interfering with ∆, in symbols S ̸⇝ ∆, when
MEV(S | ∆, †∆) = MEV†∆ (S | ∆, †∆).
     Note that the inequality ≥ in Definition 8 always holds by Theorem 2(4), so checking MEV non-
interference only requires to check ≤.
     The following example discriminates MEV non-interference from Babel et al.’ ε-composability, showing
that a contract with intended MEV but no interactions with the context enjoys MEV non-interference, but
it is not ε-composable.
Example 12. Consider the airdrop contract in Listing 3, let S be any blockchain state, and let ∆ =
Airdrop[n: T, t = T]. We have that S ̸⇝ ∆, since:

                  MEV(S | ∆, {Airdrop}) = n · $1T = MEV{Airdrop} (S | ∆, {Airdrop})

Here, MEV non-interference correctly reflects the fact that M cannot leverage the contracts in S to damage
the airdrop.                                                                                             ⋄

5.2. Sufficient conditions for MEV non-interference
    Theorem 14 gives sufficient conditions for MEV non-interference. Condition 1 states that S ̸⇝ ∆ holds
whenever the new contracts ∆ are MEV-free: a special case is when ∆ has no tokens, by Theorem 2(2).
Condition 2 allows to establish S ̸⇝ ∆ even when ∆ has MEV, provided that the token flows from S to ∆
cannot be exploited by adversaries, and that ∆ is observationally invariant w.r.t. adversarial moves in S .
Recall that this property (see Definition 5) ensures that any transaction sent by adversary to the contracts
in S is not observable by the contracts in ∆, and therefore the ability to call the contracts in S does not give
the adversary any advantage compared to calling only ∆. For instance, condition 2 is applicable to establish
MEV non-interference in Example 12, since the Airdrop contract is (trivially) observationally invariant
w.r.t. any S .

5.3. Resistance to front-running attacks
     In general, S ̸⇝ ∆ does not imply S | Γ ̸⇝ ∆. For instance, in Example 14 we showed that S | Γ ⇝ ∆,
but if we remove the Airdrop contract then M is no longer able to extract MEV from the Exchange, since
M ’s wallet is empty: hence, S ̸⇝ ∆. This means that, in general, MEV non-interference is not robust against
attacks where adversaries manage to deploy contracts Γ before ∆. Namely, assume that a user detects that
S ̸⇝ ∆, and then sends a transaction to deploy the contracts ∆. If this transaction is front-run with another
transaction that deploys Γ, then the new state S | Γ could MEV-interfere with ∆.
     Theorem 15 establishes sufficient conditions under which MEV non-interference S ̸⇝ ∆ is preserved
when the deployment of ∆ is front-run with the deployment of Γ. Condition 1 requires the contracts in
∆ to be sender-agnostic, i.e., their behaviour must not depend on the identity of the caller. Condition 2
requires that the deployment of Γ does not enable new token flows into ∆. If either condition is violated,
contracts in Γ may enable forms of token extraction that were impossible when ∆ interacted only with S .
For instance, if a contract in ∆ is not sender-agnostic, an access control check can succeed when the caller
is a contract in Γ (see Example 15). Similarly, token transfers from Γ to ∆ may unlock MEV-extraction
opportunities from ∆ (see Example 16).

7.1. Exchange
   The Exchange contract in Listing 2 does not enjoy neither MEV nor MEV∞ non-interferences w.r.t. a
generic context. For example, MEV interference may occur when the context includes a contract from which
the adversary can receive tokens needed to interact with the Exchange, e.g. an Airdrop or another Exchange.
Instead, MEV∞ interference may occur when the IPriceOracle instance upon which the Exchange depends
can be manipulated by the adversary. This happens e.g. when the price oracle is an AMM.
   When, instead, the Exchange depends on the PriceOracle contract in Listing 2, it is observationally in-
variant w.r.t. adversarial moves in the context. Therefore, MEV∞ non-interference holds by Theorem 17(3).
Note that MEV non-interference may still fail because of token flows to the adversary.

7.2. AMM
    We specify in Listing 9 an Automated Market Maker (AMM) inspired by Uniswap v2 [18, 19, 20].
The AMM stores reserves of two token types T0 and T1 ; these types and their initial reserves are defined
upon deployment. The method swap allows anyone to exchange units of T0 for units of T1 and vice-
versa, provided that the product between their reserves remains constant. More specifically, a transaction
A: swap(T0 , x, ymin ) allows A to send x: T0 to the AMM, and receive at least ymin : T1 in exchange. Symmet-
rically, A: swap(T1 , x, ymin ) allows A to exchange x: T1 for at least ymin : T0 . The method getRate gives the
exchange rate between the two token types, thereby making the AMM usable as a price oracle.
    AMMs have a well-known source of MEV when the internal exchange rate given by getRate is not
aligned with the external rate (i.e., the ratio of the token prices given by the price function $1). In this case,
MEV can be extracted by firing a swap transaction that realigns the internal with the external rate [21].

7.4. Swap proxy
    The SwapProxy contract in Listing 11 composes two exchanges, providing a swap method that forwards
the call to one of the underlying exchanges. More precisely, when swap is called, it queries the current rates
of the two underlying exchanges, and selects the one with the maximum rate to perform the swap. The
composed exchanges must implement the IExchange interface in Listing 2 (for example, possible instan-
tiations are the Exchange and the AMM contracts), and must handle the same token types. Note that, in
general, SwapProxy does not guarantee the optimal swap that one could achieve leveraging the underlying
exchanges, for various reasons. First, the queried exchange rate is only the marginal rate, i.e. the rate
obtained for infinitely small trades. In some exchanges (e.g., in the AMM), the actual rate also depends on
the input amount: swapping more tokens causes the rate to decrease (diminishing returns). Second, it may
happen that none of the underlying exchanges has a sufficient amount of output tokens to cover the input:
in that case, one could have to split the trade into two smaller trades.

7.5. Swap router
    The SwapRouter contract in Listing 12 composes two exchanges and exposes a single swap method that
enables users to perform token exchanges along a user-specified path. Each underlying exchange implements
the IExchange interface and may support swaps among multiple token types, as specified by its getTokens
method. A swap is defined by a path ⟨T0 , T1 , . . . , Tn ⟩, indicating a sequence of intermediate token conversions.
The router receives an input amount of T0 from the caller and executes a sequence of swaps Ti → Ti+1 for
i = 0, . . . , n − 1. For each hop, the router dynamically selects one of the two exchanges that supports both
tokens involved in the conversion. If no such exchange exists for some hop, the transaction reverts. The
router itself does not expose any pricing; rather, it delegates each hop to the selected underlying exchange,
approving the required token transfers. After completing the final hop, the router transfers the resulting
tokens to the caller.
    Since the balance of SwapRouter is zero in any blockchain state, there is nothing to extract, and so its
MEV is zero. Therefore, by Theorem 14(1), it enjoys MEV non-interference against any blockchain state
— regardless of the implementations of the IExchange interface. MEV non-interference also holds against
wealthy adversaries, by Theorem 17(1).

7.6. Lending Pool
    The contract LP in Listing 13 implements a simplified lending protocol that allows users to deposit a
collateral token Tc and borrow a distinct token Tb . The protocol relies on two external contract dependencies:
   • a price oracle, used to compute the value of collateral and outstanding debt;

   • an interest rate model, used to compute interests on loans (Listing 14).
   Our LP contract features the following methods:
   • deposit allows anyone to deposit tokens Tc in the pool. These tokens can be used as collateral to
     borrow tokens Tb . For simplicity, in Listing 13 we omit the method to redeem deposited Tc tokens.

   • borrow allows any user with sufficient collateral to withdraw tokens Tb from the pool. The actual
     condition is defined in the method _isColl: more precisely, borrowing is possible when the value of
     the user’s collateral is at least BT times the value of their debt (after the borrow). In our example, we
     choose a borrowing threshold BT = 150%. Note that, once tokens are borrowed, the value of the debt
     will automatically increase over time following the interest rate model.

   • repay allows a borrower to return part of their debt.
   • liquidate allows anyone to repay the debt of an under-collateralized borrower, thus gaining the
     ownership of the collateral. We consider a borrower to be under-collateralized when the value of their
     collateral is below LT times the value of their debt. In our example, we choose a liquidation threshold
     LT = 120%. Note that a borrower can become under-collateralized for various reasons, such as the
     accrual of interest on the debt and price fluctuations (e.g., the price of the collateral token Tc drops).
     For simplicity, the LP contract in Listing 13 implements a full liquidation mechanism, in which the
     liquidator repays the entire outstanding debt and receives the borrower’s entire collateral balance.
   Lending protocols of this kind are notoriously subject to MEV [14]. For example, an adversary with
the power of causing sufficiently large price fluctuations can cause a borrower’s collateral to fall below
the threshold, and then trigger liquidation by repaying the borrower’s debt and receiving its collateral in
exchange. Under suitable conditions on prices, this can cause a loss of value to the LP contract.

8. Related work

     A primary term of comparison for our MEV non-interference is the ε-composability notion of [8]:

                            ∆ is ε-composable in S          iff   MEV(S | ∆) ≤ (1 + ε) MEV(S )

    The key conceptual difference between the two notions is that ε-composability is a global property of the
entire blockchain state, whereas MEV non-interference has a more local nature, as it only requires inspecting
the newly deployed contracts and their dependencies. Indeed, the “only if” direction of the implication in
Theorems 15 and 18 states that, to establish the MEV non-interference of ∆ in state Σ | Γ, we can neglect the
part of the state Γ of the non-dependencies of ∆, under suitable conditions (e.g., deps(∆) is sender-agnostic).
    Technically, ε-composability and MEV non-interference (in both “rich” and “poor” versions) are incompa-
rable notions. To show that MEV non-interference does not imply ε-composability, assume that the newly
deployed contracts ∆ contains exactly the Airdrop contract from Example 12, with a wallet of n: T. In
S | ∆, the adversary can extract n: T from Airdrop, and possibly use these tokens to extract more MEV
from S . Therefore, we have MEV(S | ∆) ≥ n · $1T + MEV(S ), which implies that, if n is large enough, the
airdrop is not ε-composable with S . Example 12 shows, instead, that MEV non-interference holds.
    Example 18 below shows that the converse implication fails as well.
Example 18. We craft 0-composable contracts which violate MEV non-interference. For this, we define Γ
and ∆ such that both contracts expose identical and mutually exclusive MEV: one can extract MEV from
either contract, but not from both. The choice of extracting MEV from Γ or from ∆ is done by calling a

    Another difference between MEV non-interference and ε-composability is highlighted by the first item
of Theorems 14 and 17, which state that if the newly deployed contracts have zero MEV, then MEV non-
interference holds. This is not, instead, a sufficient condition for ε-composability: a contract may not be
0-composable in S when M does not have the tokens needed to extract MEV in S , but becomes able to do
so after exchanging tokens between S and ∆, provided that this preserves ∆’s wealth (see Example 19).
Example 19. Using the states S and ∆ in Example 7, we show that a contract with zero MEV may not be
0-composable with the context. Note that any action on Exchange2 does not alter its wealth, since the total
number of tokens held in that contract is preserved (because their internal prices given by PriceOracle2
are equal), and they have the same (external) price. Therefore, MEV(S | ∆, {Exchange2}) = 0, and so
condition 1 of Theorem 14 ensures that S is MEV non-interfering with ∆.
    Instead, ∆ is not 0-composable with S : indeed, MEV(S ) = 0 ̸= MEV(S | ∆) = 1, since M can first swap
her 1: T2 for 1: T1 using Exchange2, and then swap 1: T1 for 2: T2 using Exchange1, with an overall gain of
1: T2 . This is coherent with the different interpretations of composability: for [8], adding a new contract
must preserve the global MEV, while our notion requires to preserve the MEV of the victim contracts. ⋄
    The work [23] applies classical program non-interference notions to the setting of smart contracts. There,
smart contracts are specified in a general-purpose concurrent imperative language. Unlike in our approach,
where token holdings and exchanges are modelled implicitly in the contract semantics, in [23] they must be
explicitly programmed as assignments to suitable variables. Therefore, measuring the MEV of a contract
is reduced to estimating the change of such variables. Similarly, establishing non-interference amounts
to detecting whether some high-level variables (e.g., the rate of an exchange contract) can affect low-
level variables (e.g., the wallet of a contract using that exchange). Therefore, this approach requires the
programmer to identify the high-level and the low-level variables, and to manually downgrade some variables
from high to low to explicitly admit some flows (e.g., the oracle owner can update the exchange rate). Our
approach, instead, does not require the programmer to annotate the contract code: roughly, the “low”
variable is the MEV extractable from the victim contracts, and the “high” variables are the methods callable
from the adversary.
                                                         37
    This observation reveals a key difference between our approach and [23]: MEV non-interference may
hold also when the adversary can affect the victim contracts’ wallet, provided that their loss is preserved,
while in [23] any flow from the variables touched by the adversary to the wallet is considered harmful. For
example, consider a variant of the Airdrop contract that allows its users to withdraw either a token T1 or
another token T2 , where both tokens have the same external price. This Airdrop contract can only be used
once, and internally chooses which token to send by querying another contract PriceOracle. According to
our notion of MEV non-interference, there is no interference between PriceOracle and the Airdrop, since
the extracted token will have the same price, hence generating the same MEV. Instead, the non-interference
notion in [23] can observe that PriceOracle can interfere with Airdrop, affecting its behavior.
    More than with MEV non-interference, the notion of non-interference in [23] seems related to our obser-
vational invariance (Section 3.4). Informally, this notion requires that no action performed by the adversary
on S can influence what the contracts in ∆ are able to observe from S . Conceptually, this resembles a classic
non-interference property, where high-level variables (i.e., the methods of the contracts in S ) are prevented
from affecting low-level variables (i.e., the observations that ∆ can make on S ).
    The work [24] introduces a quantitative versions MEV non-interference, which enables to measure the
effect of MEV attacks on the victim contract. More specifically, given a set of victim contracts ∆ and a
system S , the value I(S ⇝ ∆) ∈ [0, 1] measures the increase of loss (proportionally to ∆’s wealth) that
adversaries can inflict to ∆ by manipulating the context S . In particular, a value I(S ⇝ ∆) = 0 means that
no such increase is possible: this is coherent with our notion, i.e. I(S ⇝ ∆) = 0 holds iff S ̸⇝ ∆. Instead,
a larger (positive) value of I(S ⇝ ∆) means that a larger fraction of ∆’s wealth can be extracted by the
adversary. The quantitative nature of this notion makes it possible to have more fine-grained results about
the effect of adding new contracts to the system. In particular, I(S ⇝ ∆) increases when S is extended
with contracts that are not in the dependencies of ∆, i.e. I(S ⇝ ∆) ≤ I(S | Γ ⇝ ∆). By contrast, our
state narrowing results (Theorems 15 and 18) can only elaborate on conditions about Γ that preserve MEV
non-interference. A potential advantage of a quantitative notion vs. a qualitative one is to understand the
effect of different system parameters on the MEV increase. The work [24] exemplifies this by measuring the
degree of interference in compositions akin to our AMM/Option and AMM/LP use cases in Section 7. However,
establishing non-trivial interference bounds in complex contract compositions seems quite more challenging
than establishing our MEV non-interference.
    A preliminary version of this work appeared in [25]. The present article substantially extends that work,
both in its theoretical development and in its analysis of DeFi compositions. On the theoretical side, we
refine the conditions under which state narrowings/widenings preserve MEV and MEV non-interference.
In particular, while [25] established local reasoning principles only for unbounded-wealth adversaries, the
current work extends these results to bounded-wealth adversaries. This yields preservation theorems for
both MEV and ̸⇝ under suitable conditions (Theorems 5 and 15, respectively), thereby providing a unified
treatment of the two adversarial models. On the practical side, the current version broadens the analysis
of DeFi compositions. In [25], the swap proxy, swap router, and arbitrage protocols were studied only in
conjunction with AMM-based exchanges. Here, we generalise the analysis to arbitrary exchanges implement-
ing the required interfaces (Table 2). In addition, the present version streamlines and generalises several
technical developments, and provides complete proofs for all results.


9. Conclusions and discussion

    We have proposed MEV non-interference, a new security notion for DeFi composition which ensures
that adversaries cannot inflict economic harm on compound contracts by exploiting their dependencies. In
particular, we have studied sufficient conditions for MEV non-interference (Theorems 14 and 17), and rules
to enable its modular verification (Theorems 15 and 18). We have shown that these rules allow to correctly
classify the composability of several common DeFi protocols (Table 2).

Pitfalls. MEV non-interference enjoys some interesting structural properties: notably, Theorems 15 and 18
show that, under certain conditions, non-interference is preserved when extending or restricting the state.
At the same time, a number of seemingly plausible structural properties do not hold in general. Table 3
summarises several such non-properties of ̸⇝∞ , which — by Theorem 16 — also fail to hold for ̸⇝.
    The first two sections show that, in general, adding or removing contracts from ∆ does not preserve
MEV non-interference. While it is intuitively obvious that adding contracts breaks non-interference, the
fact that removing them also breaks it is somehow surprising. The last section of Table 3 is even more
surprising: proving that Γ does not interfere with both ∆1 and ∆2 is not enough to guarantee that Γ does
not interfere with the composition ∆1 | ∆2 . Since the counterexamples needed to refute these conjectures
are quite technical, we relegate them to Appendix D.

Adversary model. Our definition of MEV is parameterised by a set of addresses M controlled by the ad-
versary. In principle, different choices of M may yield different MEV values and, consequently, different
non-interference classifications for the same contract composition. In practice, however, any sufficiently

                                                         39
large set of addresses that does not already occur in the contracts under analysis (i.e., neither in their stor-
age nor as hard-coded constants in their code) would yield the same MEV. It would therefore be possible
to reformulate our notions to as to eliminate any dependence on the particular choice for M, albeit at the
cost of an increased complexity of the definitions and statements.
    A similar approach has been adopted in [14] in the context of the classical interpretation of MEV
as maximal adversarial gain. There, the authors introduce the notion of universal MEV, defined as the
maximum gain that can be extracted over all cofinite sets of addresses M, after suitably redistributing
tokens among the wallets controlled by M. Besides abstracting from the actual identity of the adversary
(i.e., from the choice of the set M), this notion also abstracts from their wealth, in this second respect being
conceptually similar to our MEV∞ (Definition 7). The two notions differ, however, in the assumptions
they make about the adversary’s available tokens. In universal MEV, the adversary’s capital is ultimately
bounded by the total amount of tokens already occurring in the blockchain state (since the definition just
redistributes that to M). In contrast, our definition of MEV∞ assumes that the adversary has access to
whatever capital is required to execute the attack.
    Another key difference between our notion of MEV and that of [14] is that ours implicitly assumes an
empty mempool, while the definitions in [14] are parameterised by the mempool. In certain scenarios, access
to pending transactions in the mempool increases the adversary’s opportunities for MEV extraction. A
notable example is the sandwich attacks on AMMs [26], where the adversary observes a user’s trade in the
mempool and strategically places transactions before and after it, causing the user’s trade to execute under
less favourable conditions, while generating a profit for the adversary. Our framework abstracts away from
the mempool because our primary goal is to to study secure composability, namely the additional MEV
opportunities introduced by newly deployed contracts rather than those arising from pending transactions.
While incorporating the mempool into our definitions would be consistent with the principle of analysing
security notions under increasingly stronger adversaries (as we did when considering wealthy adversaries),
doing so would considerably increase the complexity of an already technically demanding theory, while
providing limited additional insight into the composability issues that are the focus of this work.

Token prices. Our definition of the price of a token T as $1T treats token prices as exogenous parameters,
in that such prices do not depend on the blockchain state. As a consequence, our composability notions are
parametric in the chosen price assignment, and a composition classified as MEV non-interfering under one
assignment need not remain so under another.
    One possible way to eliminate this parameter would be to internalize prices in the blockchain state, for in-
stance by designating certain contracts as price oracles (e.g., contracts exposing the interface IPriceOracle).
This would make prices endogenous, but it would also make the resulting security notions depend more
heavily on the particular oracle contracts included in the state (an on their transitions). In this sense, the
dependency on external prices would not really disappear, but would instead be shifted to a dependency on
the oracle mechanisms used to determine them.
    A more robust alternative is to rely on stricter, price-independent notions of secure composability that
avoid collapsing heterogeneous token movements into a single MEV scalar. Our observational invariance
provides one such notion: it is independent of token prices by definition (Definition 5), and it implies MEV
non-interference under the sufficient conditions established in Theorems 14 and 17. Thus, observational
invariance can be seen as a conservative, price-agnostic criterion for secure composability, particularly useful
when no canonical or stable price assignment is available.

Limitations. Some of our results rely on the assumption that dependencies are backward-closed, i.e. a
contract cannot invoke a contract deployed after itself. For example, the monotonicity result of Theorem 2(5),
which plays a key role in establishing the front-running resistance of ̸⇝∞ , fails without this assumption
(see Example 6). Backward-closure is a simplification with respect to Ethereum, where dependencies may
be dynamic and even cyclic. In particular, contracts can interact through reentrant calls, enabling attacks
that exploit existing contracts to extract additional MEV, as exemplified by the DAO attack [27]. A possible
research direction is to relax these assumptions by localizing them to the contracts under analysis. Rather
than requiring backward-closure throughout the entire blockchain state, one could investigate whether our

                                                      40
results continue to hold under assumptions that only constrain the newly deployed contracts ∆ and their
dependency graph. This would leave the surrounding state S unconstrained, allowing arbitrary pre-existing
dependencies and interactions among already deployed contracts. With this relaxation, the rest of the
blockchain state would not be constrained, making our results applicable to a wider class of contracts.
    Another limitation is that our notion of MEV measures the loss of a contract as the value of the tokens
that adversaries can remove from it. In practice, adversaries could harm contracts also by freezing tokens
without actually extracting them from the contract, as in the infamous Parity Wallet attack [28]. Refining
local MEV to take these attacks into account could be done by adapting the notion of liquidity [29].
