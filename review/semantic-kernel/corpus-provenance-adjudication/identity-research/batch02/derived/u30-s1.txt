What are the different types of DEXs and how do they work?
There are primarily 3 types of DEXs:
Order book (off-chain and on-chain):
this model is closest to that of centralized exchanges in which the system (for example, order book) compiles all orders across the various prices at which traders are willing to buy and sell at and then matches buyers and sellers in order to fulfill the trades.
Automated market maker (AMM):
AMMs leverage smart contracts to settle trades directly on the blockchain. A key feature of AMMs is the use of
liquidity pools
which are made of funds contributed by investors from the community. In return for locking in their funds, these investors earn a certain amount of interest from trading fees.
DEX Aggregators:
DEX Aggregators provide a one-stop hub that consolidates prices across multiple DEXs and aims to provide users with the best price with minimal slippage. DEX aggregators aim to make it easy for users to compare prices and often layer over other security features and algorithms that help users to optimize their trades.
Why trade on DEX?
Self-custody:
we allow you to have complete self-custody of your own trades, so any movement of tokens happens directly on the wallet that the user links to their DEX accounts.
Security:
unlike centralized exchanges that might potentially be hacked, all assets traded on DEXs are stored directly in the user's wallet, thereby providing a greater degree of security in token storage.
Privacy:
unlike centralized exchanges that require users to pass their identity verification process, users are not required to submit any personal information when they trade on DEX, thereby allowing them to trade almost anonymously.
Potentially lower fees:
unlike centralized exchanges that usually charge higher transaction fees for providing value-added service or advanced trading tools, users pay little or even zero platform fees, allowing them to keep more of their profits.
Diversity of options:
anyone can mint tokens from blockchains and create a liquidity pool for it on DEX, resulting in a myriad of options for users to choose from.
What are the possible trade-offs of using a DEX?
Riskier tokens:
offering a wide range of options and no centralized authority also means that anyone can mint a token. This might give rise to scams such as that of a 'rug pull' in which the creator of a token attempts to attract as many users to purchase the token in the hope of an benefitting from an increase in token price before running away with the money.
Liquidity:
as DEXs are reliant on the community to provide liquidity and each liquidity pool consists of separate token pairings, this can result in a lower level of liquidity available per trading pair.
On-ramps and off-ramps:
most DEXs don't allow direct fiat-to-crypto exchanges. As such, users are usually inconvenienced as they have to use a separate platform to exchange their local currency for crypto or vice versa.
Less intuitive user interfaces:
one of the biggest obstacles to getting started on using DEX is the more complicated user interface that might be confusing to a new user. Additional research might be required on the users' end to compare prices across different DEXs in the search for the most secure trades that provide the optimal price.
Why does a DEX transaction fail?
When there's network congestion, for example, on the Ethereum network, the network fee could rise sharply. When you don't have enough balance to pay such a fee, the transaction may fail.
Some tokens require a certain level of slippage tolerance when being swapped at a DEX to make sure the transaction goes through. For example, if a token requires a slippage tolerance at 3% but you set it at 1% when swapping the token, this transaction may fail.
When the market fluctuates, the level of liquidity will also change. When the total transaction amount is lower than the minimum receiving amount, the contract will stop the transaction, causing it to fail.
When a user submits multiple similar transactions but the balance is only enough for one, all the others submitted after that will fail.
When there isn’t enough liquidity in the token’s trading pool, the transaction may fail due to insufficient liquidity. This means the pool does not have enough tokens to support your swap amount or would cause excessive price impact. This usually happens when trading low-liquidity tokens or when the swap amount is too large relative to the pool size. Consider reducing the trade amount, adjusting slippage tolerance carefully, or checking the token’s liquidity and trading volume before swapping.
Honeypot token or tokens that has been blacklisted can't be traded as it can be classified as contract risks.
How can I reduce risks when trading on a DEX?
Check the token’s profile, liquidity, and trading volume before swapping. Tokens with low liquidity or low trading activity carry higher execution and price risks.
Review the token assessment information and on-chain data to better understand potential risks before trading. You can navigate to it via the risk the
risk assessment
icon.
The entry point to view the risk details
Avoid placing large orders in a single transaction. Splitting the trade into smaller amounts may improve execution success.
Set your slippage tolerance carefully. A very low setting may cause the transaction to fail, while a very high setting may result in receiving fewer tokens than expected.
Consider trading through more liquid intermediary tokens (such as ETH or USDC) when swapping low-liquidity assets.
Always make sure you have enough balance to cover network fees before confirming the transaction.
Unlimited approval gives the contract ongoing permission to spend your tokens without asking again. If the approved contract (or its upgrades) is compromised or malicious, it could transfer your token balance. Use unlimited approval only with trusted protocols, and revoke unused approvals regularly.
You can adjust the priority fee (additional fee paid to validators) to increase the speed and likelihood of your transaction being confirmed, especially during network congestion.
Be aware of MEV (Maximal Extractable Value) attacks, such as front-running and sandwich attacks. These occur when bots detect your pending transaction and place their own trades before and after it, potentially causing you to receive a worse price. Enable MEV protection if your wallet or DEX supports it, you can do it during the preset stage. MEV protection routes transactions through private relays or protected mempools to reduce the risk of front-running and sandwich attacks.
Enable the anti-MEV during the preset stage
How to speed up a DEX transaction?
When you initiate a transaction on Ethereum or other EVM networks, your network fee (also known as the gas fee) is for using the network, which is paid to miners or validators who process your transaction. If you choose a lower level of network fee for your transaction, it may take a longer time to process. There's also a possibility of network problem that'll cause your transaction to stuck and make you wait for a long time. At OKX DEX of the OKX Wallet, you can choose how fast you want to have your transaction processed by modifying the network fee for contract interaction from a slower Gwei to an average or fast Gwei. You can also maintain the slow Gwei fee and continue to wait for the network to process the transaction, or reject the contract interaction and wait for the average network fee to drop before proceeding with it.
Select Network fee to set the speed of the process
Do I still need to pay network fees when a DEX transaction fails?
When you send/transfer tokens and interact with contracts, or perform other activities on the blockchains, you must pay network fees to miners or validators for processing the transactions. Miners or validators must compute the network resources consumed during transaction processing. As a result, you're required to pay the fee regardless of whether the transaction succeeds or fails. This fee isn't charged by the wallet, so we can't refund it. Therefore, we don't recommend choosing a low level network fee to save money because it might lead to transaction failure or blockage. If the transaction is blocked, you need to pay another transaction fee to eliminate the blockage, making this an unfavorable trade.
What trade parameters can I adjust to OKX DEX, and how should I configure them?
Before confirming a trade on OKX DEX, you can adjust three parameters via the settings icon on the trade panel: slippage tolerance, network fee, and MEV protection. Each parameter has an in-app tooltip — here's a guide to help you decide when and how to set them.
1. Slippage tolerance
Slippage generally refers to the percentage difference between the price quoted when you submit a trade and the price at which it actually executes onchain. Between the moment you confirm and the moment the transaction is included in a block, the price can shift due to market movement or other users trading in the same pool. If the final execution price deviates beyond your set tolerance, the trade won't go through, protecting you from filling at an unfavorable price.
Three slippage modes are available:
Dynamic
— Automatically recommends the optimal slippage for each trade based on real-time conditions such as token price volatility and liquidity, balancing the transaction success rate with price protection.
Fixed
— your trade executes strictly at the slippage value you set.
Capped
— works like Dynamic, but execution only occurs when the optimized slippage stays at or below the upper limit you set.
2. Network fee (priority fee)
The network fee is paid to miners or validators to include your transaction in a block. A higher fee increases the chance of being picked up quickly and confirmed successfully; a lower fee may result in slow confirmation or the transaction being dropped during network congestion.
Common questions:
The fee is deducted whether the transaction succeeds or fails, because validators have already spent resources processing it. This is a common mechanism across all public blockchains and isn't specific to OKX DEX.
The priority fee shown in the interface is a reference value based on recent onchain conditions. The actual amount paid may differ slightly, since the fee market changes block by block.
Different chains use different fee mechanisms (for example, EIP-1559 on Ethereum and priority fees on Solana).
3. MEV protection
MEV (Maximal Extractable Value) refers to the profit that block producers or searchers can extract by reordering, inserting, or censoring transactions within a block. The two most common attacks faced by onchain traders are:
Front-running
— a bot detects your pending transaction and places an order in the same direction just before yours, pushing the price so you fill at a worse level.
Sandwich attack
— a bot places one order before and one after your trade, profiting from the price movement caused by your transaction.
When MEV protection is enabled, your transaction is sent through trusted nodes rather than through the public mempool, so MEV bots can't see and target it before inclusion — significantly reduces the risk of sandwich attacks. The trade-off is slightly longer confirmation times in some cases. MEV protection is especially valuable for large trades or when trading on chains where sandwich bots are active.

What's slippage? How does it impact the price?
This is usually caused by
slippage
.
Slippage is the difference between the expected price of a trade and the price at which the trade is executed. Due to the varying levels of liquidity of each platform, traders often face the risk of losing assets because of the slippage.
Slippage = (Actual execution price − Expected price) / Expected price
Common causes include:
Insufficient liquidity
Market volatility
Large transactions
The greater the slippage, the larger the potential loss.
How to avoid slippage?
Slippage can't be completely avoided, but the losses caused by slippage can be reduced. First, only trade on platforms with good liquidity and well-established infrastructure. Also, it's best to choose assets with high trading volume and high liquidity. In addition to that, instead of placing a large order directly, you can choose to trade in a smaller volume that's easier to close. If your order amount exceeds the existing liquidity, DEX can't execute the transaction. If a large order is split into several small ones, they're more likely to go through as it reduces the chances of insufficient liquidity. Next, set the maximum slippage. When the actual amount you'll receive is smaller than the amount by the maximum slippage setting, the transaction will be canceled to prevent losses. OKX DEX is a one-stop multi-chain and cross-chain aggregation trading platform. Through the X Routing algorithms, OKX DEX automatically calculates the optimal transaction path and splits orders, providing users with the best quotes with lower slippage and lower network costs.
Why do some tokens have higher slippage?
Some tokens require a high slippage, such as SafeMoon, which is used for the buyback of liquidity pool tokens, crypto burning, and for incentivizing token holders. With such tokens, OKX DEX has launched automated slippage to help users improve the success rate of transactions with optimal slippage.
How does OKX DEX find the best swap route for my trade?
OKX DEX integrates the liquidity pools of AMM and PMM protocols on each chain. It composes every possible swap route from these pools, simulates the swap outcome of each route, and selects the route with the best outcome as the optimal route.
Can a single order be split across multiple pools? What is the order-splitting logic?
While simulating the swap outcomes of each route, whenever multiple liquidity pools or routes are available, OKX DEX attempts to split the single order into multiple sub-orders routed through different pools or paths, then aggregates the simulated results. If the aggregated split result is better than the un-split result, the order is split.
How is slippage calculated on OKX DEX?
The current slippage output is based on the token pair's recent volatility. Specifically, OKX DEX collects the pair's swap rate at multiple recent time points and computes the swap-rate volatility using standard statistical methods such as log returns and standard deviation.
How is the default slippage determined? Do defaults vary across tokens or liquidity levels?
The default slippage is set with reference to comparable products in the market and to the actual price volatility of each token.
Defaults are configured differently across different tokens and liquidity levels.
How does Auto Slippage work?
Auto slippage is derived from the recent volatility of the token pair combined with fixed slippage configurations.
How is the minimum received amount (minReceive) derived from slippage?
minReceive = simulated swap output from the routing algorithm × auto slippage (or the fixed slippage set by the user).
Why might the quoted price differ from the actual executed price?
The quoted price may differ from the actual executed price due to several factors, including slippage, pool fluctuations caused by other transactions, and current block congestion together with block-packaging delays.
What is the difference between the market price and the quoted price?
The K-line price is based on the actual on-chain transaction price. The quoted price uses a unified token price, which is based on on-chain transaction prices, third-party prices, and similar sources.
